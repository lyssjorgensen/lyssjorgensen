Data Ethics in Action: What a Simple Survey Taught me

Assignment 2

Data Ethics

One of my recent assignments put me in the shoes of a data analyst. I was to be given course survey results and asked to review them and make recommendations to help figure out which course needed to be updated or redesigned. Honestly when I first read it I thought it would be pretty straightforward.

But when I actually started reading through the survey something felt off…

A Task That Seemed Simple

The survey told participants were anonymous. But to take the survey they had to log in with their username and password. I was sitting there of a second thinking wait, those two things don’t really go together. If you’re being told to log in with your credentials your credentials your identity is attached to the session. Even if the final report doesn’t show names the system could still technically connect a response to a person through things like log in records, user ids, or time stamps. That’s not really anonymous, just anonymous looking and I think there’s a difference.

The Problem With Feeling Watched

Once I noticed that I started thinking about what it would actually feel like to be the students completing that survey. You’re being asked to rate your courses and your instructors. You’re told your answer are private. But you had to log in to get there. Are you really going to be honest? Maybe. But probably not.

A lot of students will hold back. Not because they’re dishonest but because they’re not totally sure who might see their answers. Will my instructor know I gave them a low rating? Could this come back to bite me somehow? Those kinds of thoughts don’t have to be loud to be influential. Even a quiet “I’m not sure this is private” can change how someone will answer a survey.

And here’s the thing about that. It doesn’t just raise ethical concerns. It also makes the data worse. If people aren’t answering honestly the results of the survey don’t actually reflect the reality of the course. That affects instructors, students, and the quality of the program itself.

The Questions had Problems Too

Beyond the anonymity issue I have some concerns about how the questions were written. One of them asked whether the course met “all” of the course objectives. The word “all” kinda bothered me.

What if the course was great overall but fell short on one objective? Does the participant click disagree? Does that feel fair to them or the instructor? It’s the wording that can push people towards extreme answers when the reality is usually somewhere in the middle.

There was also no reminder of what the course objectives actually were. If you started the course six months ago and someone asks you today whether it met all its objectives you’re not going to remember it all, you’ll probably go off general vibe more than specific memory. That’s not a great foundation for decisions surrounding changing/redesigning the curriculum.

Things I Hadn’t Expected to Think About

Once I started getting into it, a few ither concerns came up that I hadn’t anticipated going in.

One was reidentification risk. In small classes even anonymous data can be traceable. If only eight people took a specific course and two people gave strongly negative ratings it doesn’t take much to narrow down to who it may be. The data technically have to have their name on it but the anonymity is pretty thin.

Another was the question of what happens after the data is collected. Even if the survey was perfectly designed the results could still be misused. If the administration takes a single low rating and uses it to cut a program or fire an instructor without deeper context that’s a problem. Survey data should be one input into the conversation but not the final word on something complex such as course quality.

What I Would Actually Recommend

If I were bringing this up to management I’d focus on a few practical things. First fix the anonymity issue… for real. Either make the survey genuinely untraceable or be upfront with the students about what is and isn’t private. Pretending something is anonymous when it might not be us worse than just being honest about how the data works.

Second would be to clean up the questions. Break the broad statements into smaller more specific ones, remind people what the objectives were before asking them to evaluate them. Add some open ended questions students can actually describe their experience instead of just clicking a number on a scale.

Thind think carefully about how results get reported and used. Aggregate the data. Don’t break it into groups small enough to identify individuals. Be transparent about response rates and potential limitations. And make sure whoever is reading the results understands what that data can and can’t tell them.

If No One Takes It Seriously

I thought about this part a lot actually. What do you do when you raise a concern and it doesn’t land?

I think the honest answer is that you document it anyway. You write down what you flagged, why it mattered and what you suggested. You do your part of the analysis carefully and responsibly even if the bigger system has gaps. You bring evidence rather than frustration. And if the issue is serious enough you find the right person or channel to escalate it through.

You can’t always control what an organization does with your input. But you can control how you show up professionally and what you’re willing to put your name on.

What This Actually Taught Me

I came into this assignment expecting a simple task and left it thinking about trust. About what it means to ask people for honest feedback and whether the systems we build actually deserve it. A course survey seems like such a small thing but the principals underneath it like privacy, transparency, fairness, informed consent, those show up everywhere when working with data.

We’re working with information that affects real people and that’s always going to be worth taking seriously.
