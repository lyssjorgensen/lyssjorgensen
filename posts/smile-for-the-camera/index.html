Smile for the Camera: The Ethics of Facial Recognition at Pearson Airport

Alyssa Jorgensen

Data Ethics

Every Time I Land at Pearson

I have been through Pearson Airport more times than I can count. And everytime I get off my flight to go through customs I notice the same thing. You walk up to the kiosk, scan your passport, look into the camera and answer a few questions. A slip prints out. You hand it over to a border officer. They glance at it for maybe three seconds and wave you through.

Nobody really checks. They just assume the machine got it right.

That always sticks with me because it raises a question that goes beyond convenience. When we rely on AI to make decisions about who gets into a country and border officers trust those results without question, what happens when the system is wrong? And more importantly, who is really at fault?

What’s Actually Happening at the Kiosks

The Primary Inspection Kiosks at Pearson are part of the Canada Border Services Agency’s traveller modernization program. When you arrive from an international flight you’re directed to one of these kiosks where you scan your travel document, take a photo and answer customs declaration questions. The system compares your face to the photo on your passport and generates a receipt that you take to a border officer.

What most people don’t realize is that this isn’t optional. You don’t get to choose whether your face gets scanned. The system is mandatory for almost all international travellers and that raises the first big ethical concern… informed consent.

In most cases where biometric data is collected people have the right to know what’s being collected, why and how it will be used. At the kiosk there’s no real explanation. You step up, the camera scans you and you move on. The lack of choice or transparency around the process is a significant issue when were talking about one of the most sensitive forms of data, your face.

The Pilot Nobody Told You About

This isn’t the first time Pearson has been at the center of facial recognition controversy. In 2016 the Canadian government quietly ran a project called “Faces on the Move” for six months at Pearson’s Terminal 3. Using 31 cameras that were positioned in the international arrivals area the system would capture images of travellers and compare them against a database of previously deported individuals. The program ran for about six months and approximately 2.9 million people passed through that area curing the testing period.

The weirdest and most upsetting part? The CBSA did not put up any signage informing travellers that the technology was being used. Millions od people had their faces scanned and compared against a government database without their knowledge. Minor oversight? Absolutely not. That’s a fundamental breach of transparency and its exactly the kind of situation that ruins public trust in AI systems.

Not Everyone Gets Equal Treatment

We have to mention one of the biggest and most documented problems with facial recognition technology, and its that it doesn’t work equally as well for everyone. Research across the board has consistently shown that these systems preform less accurately on darker skin tones, women and older individuals. The systems are often trained on datasets that overrepresent a certain demographic and underrepresent others which means the errors aren’t random. They fall disproportionately on people who are already marginalized.

At a border crossing the stakes if getting it wrong are extremely high. A false match could mean being pulled aside for secondary inspection, missing a connecting flight or being detained. A false rejection could create delays and stress for someone who has done nothing wrong. When those errors consistently happen to the same groups of people that’s not just a technical problem. It’s a fairness problem and reflects systemic bias baked into the algorithm itself.

This is where the concept of algorithmic fairness becomes critical. Fairness in AI means more than just accuracy on average. It means examining who the system fails and ensuring that errors are distributed equitably across different groups. Right now there is no publicly available data showing how Pearson’s kiosk system preforms across different demographics and that lack of transparency itself is an ethical issue.

Three Seconds and a Wave Through

Going back to my own experience at Pearson, what concerns me most isn’t just the technology itself. Its how humans interact with it. When a border agent glances at your slip for three seconds and waves you through they are essentially delegating their judgement to the algorithm. The human check that should exist as a safety net has become a formality.

This is what AI ethics researchers call automation bias. The tendency to over trust automated systems even when human oversight is available. In high stakes environments like border control this kins of deference to the machine can have serios consequences. If the system flags someone incorrectly and the officer doesn’t look closely enough to catch it the person suffers a consequence based on an error no one bothered to verify.

Good AI governance means keeping humans meaningfully in the loop. Not just present at the end of the process but actively engaged in reviewing and questioning the outputs of automated systems especially when those outputs affect peoples’ rights and freedoms.

Doing Better Than the Minimum

Canada has a privacy legislation including PIPEDA but the rules around government use of biometric data at borders are not as clear or as strong as they need to be. The 2016 Pearson pilot happened with no public announcement and no meaningful regulatory oversight until it was uncovered years later through a freedom of information request. That gap between what the law technically allows and what ethical practice looks like is exactly where soft ethics becomes important.

Soft ethics refers to the voluntary commitments organizations make to go beyond what regulation requires. In the context of AI at borders that might mean proactively publishing accuracy data broken down by demographic, giving travellers clear information about how their biometric data is used and stored, establishing independent review processes for AI systems used in border decisions and creating accessible mechanisms for people to challenge decisions made about them by automated systems.

Those aren’t things the law currently requires. But they’re things a responsible organization committed to ethical AI would do anyway.

A More Ethical Path Forward

The use of facial recognition at Pearson isn’t going anywhere. In fact, Canadian airports are pushing for more biometric integration across check in security and boarding. That makes it more important not less to get the ethics right now before these systems become even more deeply embedded in the travel experience.

At minimum travellers deserve to know their face is being scanned and why. The accuracy of theses systems across different demographic groups should be publicly reported and independently audited. Border officers need to be trained to actively engage with the outputs of these systems rather than treat them as final answers. And there needs to be a clear process for people to raised concerns when they believe an AI system has made an error that affected them.

The Bigger Picture

Standing at a kiosk at Pearson and looking into a camera isn’t a neutral act even if it feels like one. It’s a moment where your biometric data is captured, processed and used to make a decision about you by a system that you didn’t choose and probably don’t understand. That deserves more scrutiny than a three second glance a printout.

AI at borders can be useful. But useful and ethical are not the same thing and right now the gap between them at Pearson is wider than it should be. Transparency, fairness, meaningful human oversight and respect for consent aren’t obstacles to efficient border management. They’re the foundation that makes ant system worth trusting.
